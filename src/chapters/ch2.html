Chapter 2

Supervised learning

All the core concepts of supervised learning, illustrated with a model everyone knows: the straight line.

  • ModelAn equation defines a family of functions. The parameters select one member of that family.
  • TrainingWe look for the parameters that minimize the loss on the training data, usually by gradient descent.
  • TestingOnly held-out test data shows whether the model generalizes, underfits or overfits.
§ 2.1

The framework

A supervised learning model is a function f with parameters ϕ. The equation fixes which input/output relationships are possible at all. The parameters select one of them. Computing the prediction y from an input x is called inference.

y = f[x, ϕ]

Training searches for the parameters that give the smallest loss L on the I training pairs {xi, yi}. The loss is a scalar that summarizes how poorly the model predicts the training outputs.

ϕ̂ = argminϕ L[ϕ]Strictly speaking, L also depends on the training data. Since the data is fixed during training, only the parameters are written.

A small training loss alone proves nothing. Only a separate test set shows whether the model generalizes to examples it has not seen. The chapter also assumes structured, or tabular, data: fixed-size vectors whose elements are always in the same order. For a used car, the age always comes first, then the mileage.

§ 2.2

Linear regression: model, loss, training

The simplest model is a straight line with y-intercept ϕ0 and slope ϕ1. Every choice of parameters is one member of the family of all lines.

y = f[x, ϕ] = ϕ0 + ϕ1x

The least-squares loss sums the squared vertical deviations between the line and the data points. Squaring means the direction of the deviation does not matter, whether a point lies above or below the line. Large deviations are penalized disproportionately. Chapter 5 gives the deeper theoretical justification for this choice.

L[ϕ] = ∑i=1I (ϕ0 + ϕ1xi − yi)2

With two parameters we can compute the loss for every combination and visualize it as a surface. With millions of parameters that is impossible. Training therefore starts from a random initialization, measures the gradient of the loss at the current position, and takes a step downhill. This repeats until the surface is flat.

ϕ ← ϕ − α · ∂L/∂ϕα is the learning rate. It sets the step size.

Linear regression has a closed-form solution, which the “Closed-form solution” button uses. The book still introduces the iterative approach because it also works for models that have no closed-form solution and too many parameters to evaluate every combination. That is practically every deep network.

§ 2.2.4

Testing: underfitting and overfitting

How well prediction accuracy generalizes to test data depends on two things. The training data must be representative and complete, and the expressiveness of the model must match the problem.

UnderfittingThe model is too simple to capture the true relationship between input and output. Training and test error are both high.
OverfittingThe model is so expressive that it also describes statistical peculiarities of the training data that are atypical. Training error is low, test error is high.
Notes to chapter 2

Two pairs of terms

Loss function and cost function

The book mostly uses the two terms interchangeably. Strictly speaking, the loss function is the term associated with a single data point, one summand. The cost function is the overall quantity that is minimized and may contain additional terms not associated with any data point, such as a regularization term from chapter 9. Objective function is the general term for anything that is maximized or minimized.

Discriminative and generative models

The models in this chapter are discriminative. They predict y directly from the measurement x. A generative model goes the other way and describes how the measurement is created from the output. This makes it possible to build in prior knowledge, for example about 3D geometry and light transport when estimating the pose of a car in an image. To perform inference, however, the model must be inverted, which can be difficult.

Energy baseline of a press

A linear regression is often enough for a first, easily explainable result. The mean power consumption of a press can be modeled as a base load plus power per stroke rate.

P = ϕ0 + ϕ1 · nP mean power in kW per hour, n strokes per minute as an hourly mean

TestPrediction for weeks that were not part of training
UnderfittingTooling or material strongly affect consumption, but the line ignores them
OverfittingA very flexible model fitted to only a few shifts also learns one-off events such as a downtime

The value lies in the residual. If measured power stays above the model's expectation, this points to excess consumption, for example from hydraulic leaks or worn pumps. An example calculation with assumed values:

ΔP · t · c = 5 kW · 6,000 h/a · 0.20 €/kWh = 6,000 €/a
Consulting perspective. Both parameters are physically interpretable. That eases acceptance by the client and is a good argument for starting with a simple model. A deep network only pays off once test error and residuals show that the line no longer captures the relationship.
